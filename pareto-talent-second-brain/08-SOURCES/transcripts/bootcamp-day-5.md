## Frameworks taught

### 1. "A landing page is an offer"
Slide: "A landing page is an offer. Every section on it exists to fight one objection."
Ivan: "Offers are basically the core idea of any business... if your offer is good, you almost don't need any marketing. But if your offer is bad, even the best marketing, best website will not help it." Example: free Teslas for a video testimonial needs no marketing. Example: Hormozi's acquisition.com / gym-launch pages are "subpar" yet out-sell because the offer is better.

### 2. Grand Slam Offer: five things first (Hormozi, simplified)
Slide: "Ask Claude to interview you for each one instead of writing them cold: 'ask me 20 questions you'd need to build me a perfect offer.'"
1. **Audience**: who it is for, specifically. "Name the exact person: their business stage, their role, what a normal week actually looks like for them." (Ivan: "Who's our ICP? Ideal Client Profile.")
2. **Problem**: "The one pain they'd genuinely pay to make go away. Not a list of five problems, the single one that's keeping them up at night right now, in their own words."
3. **Solution**: "Not the tool or the feature, the real outcome they walk away with once it's working." (Ivan: perceived value must be higher than the cost.)
4. **Bonuses**: "Real value that knocks out their next objection, not a random extra bolted on."
5. **Guarantee**: "What removes the risk from their side, not yours. If it doesn't work, say plainly what happens to their money or their time."

Common mistake (Ivan): entrepreneurs "start with the solution, and they end with the solution... like, I will do websites. Okay, for whom? What is their actual problem?"

### 3. The Value Equation
Slide: "Push the top up. Push the bottom down." Value = (Dream outcome x Perceived likelihood) / (Time delay x Effort & sacrifice). "A page converts when it moves that ratio in your favour." "Every objection on your page is really just a visitor doing this math badly in their head. Fix the number, not the objection."
- **Dream outcome (push up)**: "'Save time' is weak. 'Get your inbox to zero by 9am, every day' is the same promise with the dream turned all the way up." Ivan: "They want the opposite of their problems."
- **Likelihood (push up)**: "A number beats an adjective: '312 founders placed' does more work than 'trusted by many.'" (illustrative slide copy, not a verified Pareto stat)
- **Time delay (push down)**: "'Results in 90 days' asks for patience. 'See your first draft brief tomorrow morning' asks for almost none."
- **Effort & sacrifice (push down)**: "'Complete our 12-step onboarding' sounds like work. 'Answer five questions, we handle the rest' sounds like relief, even when the process underneath is identical."

Ivan also explains cost as two parts (leaky-faucet example): the price of the fix plus the ongoing cost of the problem ("your floor is wet all the time").
Worked examples: the bootcamp itself (free, high value; dream = "skills you need to get the job of your dreams"; likelihood = "already 3,000 people went through that", "dozens of testimonials"; time delay still high because placement depends on founder demand; effort high because of homework and hours). Also a barbershop (show haircuts, Instagram testimonials, "can we schedule you for today?", 30 min vs 2 hrs).

### 4. Scarcity and Urgency
- **Scarcity**: limiting supply ("Only 12 spots this cohort").
- **Urgency**: limiting time ("Enrollment closes Friday at midnight").
- "Both work by making 'later' feel like the wrong answer. Neither works twice on the same audience if it turns out to be fake."
- Ivan: the bootcamp's scarcity/urgency "is real, it's not something that we manufacture" (limited seats, fixed start date). "If the Bootcamp was a course and not a life event, it probably would be harder to sell." "Even if you do something for free, you're still selling it."

### 5. Value Stacking ("Solve every problem, not just the one")
Three layers (slide):
1. **The product**: solves "they don't have it yet". This is the entry point.
2. **The system**: solves "they own the tool and never open it". Step-by-step instructions.
3. **The community**: solves "nobody around them is doing it too". A cohort, channel or group.
"One offer. Three problems solved. Nobody pays for volume, they pay for obstacles removed."
Ivan: stack using real, independently sold items with verifiable prices ("we are not trying to lie... We are trying to show it with the numbers"). Some buyers are emotional and some are logical, and the logical ones need the itemized stack. Rule of thumb: aim for a **10X offer** (stated value about 10x price). "Make an offer that feels scary, that you are afraid that somebody will actually buy." Keep hard costs low. Use opportunity-cost items (founder time) and one-time-built assets (PDF, checklist, framework) that "you can resell over and over."

### 6. Four ways to guarantee (slide "Deepening the fifth card")
- **Unconditional**: "No matter what, money back. The strongest signal of confidence." (Ivan: "100% satisfaction guarantee"; "some people take advantage of this stuff.")
- **Outcome-based**: "Tied to the actual result... 'We get you to X, or we keep working for free until we do.'" (Ivan's example: if you don't get 20 hours a week back, money back.)
- **Conditional**: "Money back if specific conditions are met. Do the work, get the guarantee. Filters for real effort over refund-hunting." (Ivan: e.g. "within 7 days...")
- **Anti-guarantee**: "All sales final, stated on purpose. Used rarely. Signals scarcity and exclusivity rather than confidence." (Ivan: "I'm so sure that you will get the result that I don't even need to guarantee that.")

### 7. MAGIC naming formula (Hormozi)
Slide: "Five components. Hormozi's own advice: use three to five of them, not all five forced into one name."
- **M - Magnet**: "An attractive theme or reason. Gives the name a hook beyond just describing the thing."
- **A - Avatar**: "Who it's for, right in the name. No guessing whether this applies to you."
- **I - Interval**: "The timeframe. A number in the name does real work."
- **C - Container**: "The format word. Bootcamp, Blueprint, System, Challenge, whatever names the vehicle."
- **G - Goal**: "The outcome, named directly. Not the process, the destination."
Live example: "**Get your life back** (goal/magnet) **from your business** (avatar) **in 10 weeks** (interval) **challenge** (container)."

### 8. Landing page vs funnel vs website (slide)
- **Landing page**: "One page, one goal. The first thing people see after clicking your link, your ad, or your listing. Nothing pulls them away from the one action."
- **Funnel**: "A sequence of pages where each next step only unlocks once you act on the one before it. Submit name and email, then a survey, then a thank-you."
- **Website**: "A multi-page site... serves several goals at once."

### 9. "58% never reach the form" (hero-first rule)
Slide: Microsoft Clarity (free heatmap/scroll tool) "shows this on real pages over and over: fewer than six in ten visitors ever scroll far enough to see your form." "That is not a copy problem lower on the page. It is a hero section problem." "Assume nobody scrolls." "The hero section is not the top of the page. For most visitors, it is the whole page."

### 10. Anatomy of a page that converts ("Every section fights one objection")
1. Hero: the promise and the one action
2. Social proof: names, logos, a number, right after the promise
3. Problem: "Name the pain in their words"
4. How it works: three or four steps; "Removes the fear of the unknown"
5. Benefits: what changes for them
6. More social proof: a second wave, deeper in
7. The offer: what they get, spelled out plainly
8. Guarantees: "Take the risk off them and put it on you"
9. Final CTA: "The same action as the hero. Ask again now that every objection is answered."

### 11. Vibe coding
"Describe what you want. AI generates the code." "You are the architect, not the bricklayer." Reality check: the first attempt gets about 70%, expect 3-5 iterations, "People who expect 70% ship something today. People who expect 100% give up on attempt two." Buildable: landing pages and funnels, forms and surveys (intake, qualification), simple games and puzzles ("Quizzes, calculators, anything playful enough that people finish it"), dashboards, internal tools, custom portals. Ivan: "if you will ask for stupid stuff, you will get stupid stuff." Use AI "as a multiplier of what you're already doing, not as a replacement of yourself." If the output is bad, "it means that prompt sucks... rebuild the prompt."

### 12. Branding without a designer
- **Chicken vs egg**: "Coca-Cola's logo is famous because Coca-Cola is huge, not the other way around." The first logo is an "MVP, a minimum viable logo." Ray-Ban and Chanel are wordmarks, so "A clean wordmark is a real logo."
- A brand = **logo + colors + fonts** (Ivan: "Each brand is three things").
- **80% inspiration, 20% creativity** ("good artists borrow, great artists steal"; "add your own ten percent").
- **Colors**: choose 3. Use the **60-30-10 rule** (60 filler, 30 secondary, 10 accent). "The easiest way to screw up things is to add way too many colors." Tool: Coolors. Take a palette that already works and tweak it darker or brighter.
- **Fonts**: logo font can differ from web font. Use Google Font Pairs. Serif = "established and capital", two sans-serifs = "bold and modern".
- **Images**: never from Google Search. Story on slide: a student pulled an image from a Britannica page and got a cease-and-desist "within 48 hours." Use Pexels, Unsplash, Freepik, Flaticon in sets, emojis, or AI images (ChatGPT, Midjourney, Freepik AI, Ideogram).
- "Iterate. Don't start from blank."

### 13. Build pipeline (slides)
- **Lovable** (or GoHighLevel "AI Studio", which Ivan calls "basically stolen lovable"): click Publish for a preview URL.
- **Claude Code + GitHub + Cloudflare Pages**: "This is the real pipeline behind paretotalent.com itself." Daily workflow: pull, make the change, build locally, commit and push, live in 1-2 minutes. "Lovable is a furnished apartment. Claude Code is the empty lot with the deed in your name."
- Ivan's live method: record the offer brainstorm, then give Claude (with the second brain connected) the transcript and a whiteboard photo plus "use Alex Hormozi's $100M Offers frameworks... ask me questions... break this down into the sections for the website". Next, add inspiration (full-page screenshot via the Awesome Screenshot extension, a mood board, fonts and a logo) and ask for a **master prompt** covering brand, logo, art direction, palette, typography, components, photography, motion, page structure and content per section, plus a "wow effect". Paste it into the builder.
