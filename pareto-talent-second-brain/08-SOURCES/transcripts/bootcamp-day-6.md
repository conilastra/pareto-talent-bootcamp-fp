## Topic of the day
The landing page built on Friday (Day 5: Lovable, AI Studio, Claude Code) is only one box in a longer chain. Day 6 covers **what happens after someone clicks**: finishing the hero section, then wiring the page into **GoHighLevel (GHL)** with the Form Builder (including a conditional-logic qualifier), a pipeline of opportunities, tags, automation workflows, email sequences, payments (Stripe), and nurture/remarketing. Slide thesis: "A page is one box in a longer chain. Everything after the click is what actually runs the business." In Ivan's words: "our website is not just presentation of something... our website is the conversion machine."

## Session outline (slides "Today's Plan")
1. Finishing your page, and what happens after (hero section review, live critique of homework sites, live AI Studio fixes)
2. Pipelines, opportunities, and the Form Builder
3. Automations, real sequences, and smarter triggers
4. Stripe, integrations, and payments
5. Nurture, remarketing, and a live build

## Frameworks and models taught

### 1. The hero section decides everything
- Slide stat: "58% of visitors never scroll past the hero section. Every pipeline, every automation, every email sequence today is built on the assumption that the hero actually worked."
- Ivan: "you're trying to compress the whole website just to this one screen." People "scan it in, like, half a second" and "if they don't see what they are looking for in one second, they will just leave."
- **Slide "What a working hero actually needs: four things, no exceptions"**
  1. **Title**: "The outcome, stated directly. Not clever, not vague. Someone skimming for two seconds should know exactly what this is." In the transcript: a strong title "focused on your audience's pain points."
  2. **Sub-headline/Description**: "One sentence or short paragraph, right under the title. Who it's for, or how it works, whichever removes more doubt faster."
  3. **Social proof**: "Visible before the fold, not buried below it. A number, a logo, a face. Trust stated up front, not earned later."
  4. **Video or imagery**: "Real, not generic stock. Motion earns attention a static image can't, if it's used once and used well."
- **Clarity over creativity**: "if you have a choice between clarity and creativity, I recommend you to choose clarity... Creativity is usually not something that we can easily monetize." "Go for clarity first and then you will be able to make it creative." "Ensure that you cannot be misunderstood."
- **Reading order**: visitors read the title first, then the button, then the video, and only then the rest of the text.
- **Angles**: "each message has multiples of ways for us to deliver it. It's called angles." Example for the Second Brain Workshop: a mechanics angle ("Build your second brain with Claude") versus an emotional angle ("don't forget things anymore" / "don't let work slip through the cracks").
- **Squint test** (design heuristic): squint at the page and the brightest, highest-contrast element should be the CTA button. "Call to action is the most important thing ever... We just want people to click the button."
- **Dribbble proof-of-concept rule**: before using an unusual design idea (e.g., a patterned background), find one site that does it well. "If you cannot find anyone doing that, either you're a genius or it's a bad idea... I usually recommend to think that it's a bad idea."
- **Display titles**: titles should have tighter spacing than body text and use a display font. Ivan showed this on a participant's title set in Cormorant Garamond.
- **Don't repeat card styles**: "you need to hook people all the time... otherwise it's way too repetitive, and it feels like you're reading a book... not a lot of people like reading."
- **One primary CTA**: one participant's hero had a CTA, a secondary CTA and three pills that also looked like buttons. Ivan: "it can be distracting... don't try to overcomplicate this."
- **Storytelling sites** (scroll journeys) work for art but are "sometimes... bad if we are talking about the conversion". They can make sense "if we want to educate somebody before they actually jump on the call". Main point: "balance the creativity with the actual commercial intent."
- **Rich content beats an AI-looking page**: a custom video of Kasim makes the page "feel like something real, because, hey, like, somebody sat down and spent their time. They didn't just generate this website."

### 2. Where social proof comes from (slide)
1. **Ask directly, on the spot**: "The easiest real review is the one you ask for right after a genuine win. A Google review link sent in the moment beats a generic request sent later."
2. **A deal for the testimonial**: "Early on, real proof is thin. Consider offering a discount, an extra deliverable, or extended support in exchange for an honest testimonial." (Transcript: "It can be a gift card.")
3. **A badge, not a paragraph**: "A star rating, a review count, a client logo. Social proof works fastest as something scanned in half a second, not read." (e.g., "5 stars, 100 reviews")
4. **No reviews yet? Use activity**: "An Instagram full of real client work, a follower count, a results screenshot." Transcript adds: how many people you've served, how many people interacted with the offer, "Did you work with somebody famous?"

### 3. The funnel chain (slide "The whole funnel, in one line")
**Traffic -> Landing page -> Form -> CRM**
- **Traffic**: "An ad, a post, a link someone actually clicks. This is the one box in the whole chain that lives outside GHL entirely." "Without traffic, whatever you build is absolutely meaningless." Paid ads are "the easiest way... But at the same time, it's the most expensive one". An existing audience (YouTube, Instagram) is "a much better way to launch".
- **Landing page**: "One offer, one action. If the hero doesn't answer their first objection, nothing below it gets read." Purpose 1 is to present information. Purpose 2, "the main one", is conversion.
- **Form**: "A lead magnet, a scheduling form, or checkout. The exact moment control passes from the page to your CRM."
- **CRM**: "Where the email address becomes a tagged, staged, trackable contact."

### 4. Conversion, defined
- "You have one state. And then you're moving to another state... You were empty, you became full." Examples: sale, lead (form submitted), even a click. "Primarily... we are looking for something with a commercial intent." A lead is "a conversion, but with a delayed gratification."
- "Whenever you're building a website, always think about the conversion. What will be the conversion event for this website?" Options for Kasim's workshop: enrollment (buy on the spot), a meeting, sign-up, attending a group call.
- **The low-ticket vs high-ticket rule**: the sample offer costs about $10,000 (Ivan: "our check for this event was, what, $10,000, or something like that?"), so "what is the chance that somebody will open a landing page and on spot pay $10,000 or $3,000?... pretty low." That is why you build a funnel: visit website -> call -> buy on the call -> happy customer. Direct-to-checkout is for "low-ticket items... below $300, I would say." Above about $300, "I would recommend to have some more education or a call." It also depends on the item's complexity.

### 5. The qualifier form (double conversion event)
- Flow: landing page -> **qualifier form** -> conditional redirect. **Qualified ("yes") -> booking page with Kasim's calendar.** **Not qualified ("no") -> thank-you page** ("sorry, I don't think you're a good fit for us"), planned to include a video of Kasim.
- "Now we have... double conversion event": the form submission is one event and the booking is the second. Track both.
- Why qualify: if you run Instagram ads to a cold audience, "somebody who's never seen Kasim before", few of them are good leads, and "us giving them our time is already a very limited resource."
- Qualifier ideas from the room: **income/revenue**, **company size** ("if their company size is one person, then they cannot delegate to anyone"), expected outcome (rejected because it's plain text and can't be branched on). Ivan doubted that industry, audience or age would help.
- **Conditional logic** = "if X, then Y". Use dropdowns or radio buttons (a limited set of answers), not free text.
- Revenue dropdown built live: "What is your approximate annual revenue?" with options **<$100K/yr, $100K-$250K/yr, $250K-$500K/yr, $500K+**. Under $100K redirects to /not-a-fit. Everything else goes to /book.
- Other fields: full name (one field, not first + last), phone ("What is the best phone number to contact you?"), email ("What is the best email to reach you?").
- **Consent checkboxes**: rules differ by US state and country (GDPR in Europe). "You will be primarily working with US founders. So in your case it's much easier." In most cases, remove the checkbox and cover consent in the site's terms/privacy policy. Use plain language and skip the long standard template. Only use explicit acceptance for "much more aggressive marketing".
- **Match the form to the site**: same brand color, white text, rounded button (radius 50), title case.
- **The button copy is a CTA, never "Submit"**: "Submit is very passive, it doesn't explain what happens next." Use "Reserve your spot", "Secure your spot", "Join the waitlist", "Book your meeting".
- Embed: GHL form -> Integrate -> copy embed code -> paste into Lovable/AI Studio/Claude. Prompt used: show the form as a pop-up when the button is clicked, and "explain to them what happens next, and also add some social proof."

### 6. CRM inside HighLevel (slide)
- **Contact management**: smart lists and tags. "Every person who's ever touched a form lives in one place."
- **Multi-channel comms**: SMS, email, WhatsApp and social from one contact record. "One thread, not five apps."
- **Reporting**: customizable dashboards.
- **Pipeline**: opportunities, stages, statuses.
- Contact record layout: submitted info on the left, the conversation history in the middle, the activity history on the right (pages visited, forms, workflows, client-portal engagement).
- Don't vibe-code a CRM: "you will realize that you need to build yourself a full CRM... your website became a full app... it will be a smarter idea to just sell this software." Vibe-coded forms need a database and automation hooks, so "it doesn't make sense in majority of cases."

### 7. Opportunities and pipelines
- Slide: "A contact is a person. An opportunity is that person's specific journey toward a specific outcome, sitting in a pipeline at a named stage. The same contact can have more than one opportunity." Analogy: "A contact is an entry in your phone. An opportunity is the specific deal you're actually working with them."
- **Slide pipeline (event funnel)**: 01 Registered -> 02 Attended -> 03 Booked a call -> 04 Client -> 05 Churned / Lost ("Still worth tracking, not deleting").
- **Live-built pipeline for Kasim's sample offer**: New Lead -> Qualified -> Booked -> Showed Up -> Enrolled.
- **Name stages in the past tense**, as milestones already hit ("Booked call"), not as actions ("Call", "Message").
- **Automating each stage**: New Lead is automated on form submit. Qualified is automated from the revenue answer. Booked is automated with the calendar as the trigger. Showed Up is hard to automate, so do it manually (connecting Fathom to Zapier is possible but "way too much"). Enrolled is automated from the GHL payment.
- **Conversion-rate thinking (drop-off diagnosis)**: Lead -> Call Booked -> Showed -> Money. A share of people drops off at each step, and "the idea of any business and all businesses is to increase this percentage."
  - Lead -> Booked drop-off: technical issues (404), no calendar time that works, low commitment / they don't understand what they're getting.
  - Booked -> Showed: reminders. Confirm right away by WhatsApp ("does this time work for you? I'm super excited to speak with you tomorrow"). Use **reciprocity**: give resources on the thank-you page.
  - Showed -> Paid: a better deck, a better sales process, price expectations.
- The bootcamp's own pipeline is the same idea: application step 1 -> step 2 -> interview -> live call -> final project -> join the team. "Our pipeline is, like, very long... in our case it's actually a benefit because... people who actually make it through the full pipeline, actually a good fit."

### 8. Automation building blocks (callback to Day 4)
- **Trigger**: "the event that starts everything. Here, that's almost always a form submission."
- **Condition**: "a check before anything fires." (GHL: the If/Else step)
- **Action**: "Tag the contact, move the pipeline stage, send an email."
- **Delay**: "an optional pause." (GHL: the Wait step)

### 9. The graded workflow (slide "One form submission, five real actions")
"This is the exact automation graded on the final project."
1. **Form submitted** (trigger)
2. **Tag assigned** ("labeled automatically, no manual sorting")
3. **Opportunity created** (pipeline stage updates)
4. **Internal notification** ("You find out immediately")
5. **Email sequence starts**

**The five-beat email sequence (graded on final project)**:
1. **Thank You**: sent instantly, confirms the registration
2. **Hype**: a few days out, value plus the agenda
3. **Reminder**: closer to the date, "a practical nudge, not a sales pitch"
4. **Launch Day**: the day of, "short and direct, the actual link to show up"
5. **Urgency**: hours after launch, "last call for anyone who registered but hasn't shown"

### 10. Live GHL workflow build (Ivan's version)
- Workflow name "01 Form Submitted". Trigger: Form Submitted, **filtered to your specific form**. "This is the stupidest mistake that for some reason everyone makes inside of the high level. And this is something that I disqualify people for the final project all the time." An unfiltered trigger fires on everyone's forms.
- **Step 1 is always add a tag.** Tag nomenclature: `product::action` with initials (e.g., `form::qasim_step1_IB`, `calendar::02_booked_IB`). In real accounts, tags look like "Claude Code Level 3 access", "VC Inc..." and "VAP compensation". The VibeCode Incubator account has 138 tags.
- **Segmentation via tags** ("segregation"): Ivan once had to "manually select a couple thousand records" because he hadn't tagged. Real example: filter the Pareto Talent smart list for tag BC7 approved AND tag BC6 denied = "exactly the 10 records".
- If/Else on the revenue custom field ("revenue is not less than 100K"). Qualified branch: **Wait 10 minutes** so they can book ("If I hit you directly... I will distract you from booking") -> If/Else "has tag calendar::booked?" -> if not, send email.
- Ivan prefers a tag as the "did they book" check over the pipeline or calendar, because it stops automations from misfiring.
- Email uses the custom value {{contact.first_name}}. Draft (Ivan called it "bad examples of the copy, good examples of the workflow"): from Kasim, subject "Why do you hate me?", "Thank you so much for your interest in our program, but you forgot to make the most important step, is to book a call directly with me... Or, if none of these times work for you, just reply to this email." Loop copy/paste with a further 10-minute wait, optionally adding SMS ("can you actually book?").
- Forgotten step, fixed live: **Create Opportunity** (name = contact name, source = form, status = open). Qualified people go into the **Qualified** stage, not New Lead.
- Testing: Enrollment History shows where a contact sits in the workflow. Click the contact to skip the wait step.
- Other common actions: assign tag, change opportunity, send internal notification ("there's some dude who's just submitted a form... Do you want to call them?").
- Ivan also mentioned that GHL has an AI workflow builder. "I never used it."

### 11. The thank-you page is the most neglected step
"A lot of people disregard that... this is one of the most important steps because you have somebody who went through the form, through the booking." Show case studies and testimonials "so they will not have buyer remorse", explain how to prepare for the call, share socials, build hype, and start emails. "A lot of people don't do anything after somebody submits a form. This is a fatal mistake."

### 12. Payments (slide; barely covered live)
- Connect Stripe once, then GHL can collect one-time payments or subscriptions on a form or checkout page, "no separate cart tool needed." A successful charge can trigger the pipeline move. Ivan: you can connect PayPal or Stripe, and you can charge people to book a call or send them an invoice.
- **VIP Upsell Pattern**: "One core offer, one single upsell shown right after checkout. Not five upsells, one. The person just said yes to something, this is the moment they're most open to a related next step."

### 13. Three sequence types + remarketing (slides)
- **Welcome sequence**: "Automated, three to seven emails over the first week... Job: introduce, not sell yet."
- **Newsletter**: weekly or monthly. "Job: stay present, so you're not a stranger when they're finally ready."
- **Product launch**: pre-launch, launch, post-launch. "Job: build anticipation deliberately, then create real urgency."
- **Remarketing workflows** ("the one people skip"): "A completely separate workflow, watching for exactly one thing: someone who registered or engaged, then stopped. The goal changed from informing to re-engaging." Trigger: anyone who never earned the **Engaged Lead** tag a set number of days after the original sequence ended.
- **Nurture** (transcript): "moving somebody towards the action that we want". This is why the Bootcamp funnel asks only for name + email first, "so if you forget, if you decide that it's way too much, I can send you emails, I can remind you." It is also called "email remarketing or nurturing campaigns."
- **Pareto's real Lead Nurture workflow** (shown live, Pareto/VibeCode account): a separate workflow with no trigger of its own. Other workflows end by pushing the contact into it, "so I don't have to rebuild my whole email sequence each time." It is "email delay, email delay, email delay" with **7-day delays**, so "I'll be emailing you the whole year." It exits anyone holding certain conversion tags. Another workflow shown ("scheduled a call") pushes a conversion event to Facebook, sends an internal notification, makes API calls, creates a tag and an opportunity, then hands off to nurture. Workflow names glimpsed: "client facing automation", "Technical founder with MagNet" (sic, likely a lead-magnet workflow).

### 14. Email metrics worth watching weekly (slide)
| Metric | Benchmark |
|---|---|
| Open rate | 20-40% ("Below that, the subject line is the problem") |
| Click rate | 2-5% |
| Conversion rate | "The number that pays for the other two" |

| List growth rate | new vs lost subscribers |

- **Trigger / Condition (If/Else) / Action / Delay (Wait)**: the automation blocks.

- **Internal notification**: an alert to yourself ("notify YOURSELF, not the entire HighLevel workspace").
- **Engaged Lead**: a tag for anyone who opens or clicks.
- **Nurture campaign / remarketing workflow**: see section 13.

## Homework (Day 6, exact from slide)
"Put your funnel together, start to finish, for the sample entrepreneur brief.
- Create a CRM pipeline of 5 distinct stages that map the real journey from lead to conversion.
- Using the Form Builder, create a form and embed it into the landing page you built Friday.
- Build the automation workflow triggered by that form:
  - Assign a contact tag automatically on submission.
  - Create an opportunity and set it to the correct pipeline stage.
  - Send an internal notification so you know the moment it happens. (Make sure to notify YOURSELF, not the entire HighLevel workspace)
  - Send at least two sequenced emails: a confirmation, then a follow-up on a delay.
- Test the whole chain yourself before submitting.
- Optional stretch: tag anyone who opens or clicks as an Engaged Lead.
- Share the link to your finished workflow."
Submit to: bootcamp.paretotalent.com/homework. Post in #Homework and share one thing you liked about today.

Clarifications from Q&A: the pipeline can be for your own site if you didn't build the Kasim one. The site does not need to be on GHL; embed the GHL form code into Claude artifacts, Lovable, etc. No app, database or deployment is required.
