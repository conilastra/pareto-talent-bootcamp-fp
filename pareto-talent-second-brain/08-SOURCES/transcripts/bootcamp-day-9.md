## Topic of the day

The "classic" executive-assistant core skills: inbox, calendar, project management, and closing the loop with reflection and SOPs. Ivan says this "used to be a core of the Bootcamp" when they started. It used to take 2-3 classes and is now compressed into one, the day before the last.

Core thesis (slide + Ivan): **"You are not being hired to answer email. You're hired to close loops."** Ivan: "any AI can answer emails... You're being hired to close the loops." Every tool (inbox, calendar, project board) exists "so nothing the founder said or promised quietly falls through the cracks."

1. Inbox Zero: closed loops, Claude vs Shortwave, and real features

## Block 1: Inbox Zero

### Definition (as taught)
- Slide: "Inbox Zero: Every communication you started, finished." "Not zero emails. Zero open loops."
- Slide quote: "Inbox Zero means ensuring we finish all communications we started. We are not letting them sit, we are on top of them."
- An inbox is a literal in-box: things placed in front of you to process. Open loops include a promise on a call, a Slack thread, a comment, "anything you owe a reply or an action on."
- Physical mail analogy: wrong way is letting the stack sit on your desk. Right way is to decide as you check: important items go "in a safe drawer where it lives", the rest gets thrown away. Most people never carried this over to email: "a lot of people still, even here, have 20,000 of emails in their inbox." They treat the inbox "as, like, notifications."
- The end state: everything important already replied to, everything needed later saved or archived, the rest removed or unsubscribed from.
- If you can't close a loop yet (e.g. a proposal request), use the **four C's of communication** to acknowledge it: "hey, I received your request, thank you so much, I will try to reply later today." That is proactive communication.

### Platform decision: why Gmail wins
- Gmail wins because of its **open API**, not its interface. "You can connect Gmail almost to any AI tool." Outlook, Yahoo and Apple Mail can connect, but those integrations are "not as robust."
- Stat (Pareto experience): "approximately like 15 to 20% of entrepreneurs will not use Gmail... Everyone else uses Gmail."
- Slide fallback order for Outlook founders: (1) migrate to Gmail if they'll allow it; (2) forward Outlook to Gmail and use Gmail as the processing hub; (3) otherwise use **Spark Mail**, "the best multi-platform fallback with real AI features."

### Shortwave (Pareto's recommended inbox)
- "Shortwave is what we are using here in Pareto Talent. Shortwave is what I recommend to all of the executive assistants. Shortwave is what I mentioned to the clients."
- Superhuman is "pretty good as well" for rapid cleanup, but Shortwave has many features on the **free plan**.
- Shortwave replaces the Gmail interface but stays connected to the Gmail account. To connect to Claude you connect the "naked Gmail account", not Shortwave.
- Features (slides + demo):
  - **"Help me sort my inbox"**: one button reads every thread, stars what's important, marks the rest done, offers bulk unsubscribe (demo: PayPal), and builds to-dos (demo: PayPal setup, Facebook ads, invoice, "Approve GoHighLevel account transfer for clients"). You can skip or redirect any single suggestion.
  - **Bundles and labels**: group related threads under a label and clear them all at once. Set a rule once (demo: event attendees grouped automatically).
  - **To-dos with real context**: click a to-do and every related email opens together. You can add a description. Ivan's assistant Ampy (Amparo Linares) uses this to explain what each email is and what he needs to do.
  - **Context-aware replies**: ask about a thread and it summarizes who the person is and the client journey ("even without the second brain"), then drafts "in my voice" because it learned how he writes.
  - **Calendar in the same breath**: it found an open slot on a packed Tuesday and drafted the invite to "Phoebe" (5pm Tuesday, "Do not send it yet"). Ivan: "that used to be your job description... you need to find the time, you need to answer the emails. Okay, done. Couple of minutes."
  - **Intent search**: e.g. "Find the email where I discussed buying plane tickets." "If you ever search in Google, if you don't know the exact keyword, you are kind of screwed."
  - **Multiple inboxes**: switch accounts with Ctrl+number. Ivan has 2 personal and 3 corporate accounts. A Right Hand can manage their own inbox, the founder's and a personal one in one place.

### Cut-off date strategy (for 10,000+ unread)
- "We cannot like sort through 20,000 emails, like it will consume probably my whole life."
- Transcript version: propose archiving everything older than **30 days**. If the founder pushes back, offer 60, then 90, until they're comfortable. Then bulk-archive. You're left with "a couple hundred" or "a couple thousand." (The slide says "60 days back is typical" and "If it mattered, it comes back.")
- Then find the common senders: "80% of the emails are coming from 20% of senders." Bulk-archive or unsubscribe them (PayPal and CRM notifications).
- **Archive is not delete**: it only removes the email from the main view and it stays searchable.
- For anxious founders, use **Google Takeout** to export all mail first. "Our idea is to try to minimize any obstacles that entrepreneurs might have during the sharing the inbox access with you."

### Per-founder rule book
- A living document per founder. "Every surprise becomes a permanent rule."
- Examples: "A notification from Amazon is received, then just archive it. Unless it's a failed delivery attempt." "If the email from the client comes in, prepare the draft. If the email from the client comes in and the client is pissed. Notify me immediately." The slide adds: "LinkedIn connection request, filter it out."
- Why write it down: you won't remember everything, and "Hopefully, you will escalate within the organization, so you will not be managing the inbox anymore. And you want to have the rules for the next person." "Everything that you do, you should document."

### Framework: 3 Stages of Inbox Access (trust ladder)
1. **Stage 1, Forwarding**: the founder forwards a single email they need help with. The RH researches it and comes back with a draft. Use this when the founder won't give access yet.
2. **Stage 2, Delegated access** (Gmail Settings > Accounts > "Grant access to your account"): the RH removes spam and prepares drafts but doesn't send. Payoff: if a draft is wrong, the founder deletes it and loses nothing. If it's semi-good, "It saves me like 80% of time." If it's spot on, they click send, "And in majority of cases, I just click send."
3. **Stage 3, Reply to ~90%**: once the RH has learned the rules, they reply to about 90% of emails and draft the uncertain 10%. "At this stage, you will save your entrepreneur, like, 99% of managing their inbox."
- Founders usually move 1 → 2 → 3 "through building trust and building the reputation." Some jump straight to stage 3.
- The best access is the **password**: "If you're acting as a true executive assistant, as a true right hand, the best way for you to have access is to have password." It also gives access to the calendar and tools connected to Gmail. Ask the founder to remove personal content and change to a strong password first.

### Security (liability for the RH)
- "You are taking on yourself a big chunk of liability if something goes wrong."
- Story: at least one Pareto case where a Facebook account was hacked (weak password or phishing). Whoever has the account can run ads from it.
- Rules: 2FA on every account (SMS, Google Authenticator, or passkey). Check passwords on **Password Monster** ("with AI, it's seconds" to crack). Use one master password plus a password manager: **Bitwarden** (free, open source), or Apple Keychain (which Ivan now uses). For strong passwords, use full sentences with spaces ("space acts as a symbol"), e.g. a song line or movie quote.

## Block 2: Calendar management

"The same closed-loop thinking, applied to time instead of email." Calendar work is "playing Tetris with somebody's time."

- Ivan's calendar: gray = blocked time to do work, blue = calls. Kasim "is not acting as an active operator", so his calendar is more relaxed. Nuno (another business partner) "is on meetings the whole time... no breathing room." To reach Nuno, Ivan has to go **async** (message, video or voice note; he replies whenever).

### Framework: Six calendar strategies (slides)
1. **Time blocking**: reserve real time for one thing and treat it like a meeting. Blocks also stop booking links from booking over that time.
2. **Protect deep work**: 60-90 minute blocks marked busy and color-coded. Founders "start acting as operators" doing client catch-ups and delivery. They should work "on the business" (marketing and content, sales, strategy): "That's all they should be doing. Everything else we need to be taking away from them. And the hardest part is that they will not want that." "Entrepreneurs try to hold to all of this stuff because it's again their baby. They build it, they're very afraid that somebody will break it."
3. **Mirror events for notes** ("ghost" events): a private duplicate event at the same time, without the guest, with prep notes in the description. Example: the call with Gaspar to walk through Facebook ads and prepare the lead magnet for launch. Why: founders have events "that they will have no idea how it got there", e.g. "meeting with Connor and Nate."
4. **Color-code by type**: gray for blocks; blue, green or yellow for active items.
5. **Buffer time**: pre-call buffer (~10 min before) and post-call buffer (~10 min after). Ivan has had days of "15 calls, back to back." Sales-call story: "you're ready to pay, and I tell you, I have to jump, sorry... It's ridiculous." Ask the founder their preferences. Story: one Pareto client never has more than 3 meetings a day, "even if there is time... he's too exhausted from communication with people."
6. **Weekly review and batching**: plan next week on Friday "like a puzzle" and cluster similar meetings. Ivan does all individual team check-ins on Wednesday and group calls on Friday ("Pareto Weekly", "Pareto Town Hall", "Delegation Mastermind", "VC Inc."). Protect big blocks: a stray "15 minute call with George... breaks my perfect streak" of 3 hours of maker time.

### Time zones
- The US has four continental time zones (Pacific, Mountain, Central, Eastern) plus Hawaii and Alaska. Some observe daylight saving time and some don't.
- Write **2 PM EST / 2 PM PDT**, not 14:00. "If you will be writing like that, you will be either considered military, or you will be considered as outsider."
- Pareto team spread: Nuno (New Jersey or Madeira, Portugal), Kasim (Arizona), Ivan (Montevideo), Kayla (San Francisco). That's four time zones for one real weekly Friday call. "Horrible."
- Tools (slide "Time zones, solved three ways"): **WorldTimeBuddy**, phone and desktop **widgets**, and the **Google Calendar second time zone** setting (labelled, e.g. "Kasim").
- "Is it your Monday or my Monday?" Ivan calls asking this "lazy." Assume they mean their own time zone and look it up yourself. Example: the Pareto Talent website footer gives "Pareto Talent LLC... Scottsdale, Arizona" → MST. "This is what will make you exceptionally useful. This is where nobody else will be doing that... This is what will make you stand out."

### Framework: Six rookie mistakes (slides)
1. Booking in the wrong time zone ("Confirm it out loud, every single time"). "I don't think there's a single right hand that haven't."
2. Back-to-back everything.
3. Treating the calendar as a record (logging the past instead of deciding the future).
4. Verbal confirmation with no invite sent ("A time nobody locked in isn't real yet").
5. Missing the daylight saving shift. **Arizona (Pareto HQ) does not observe DST.** Dates change yearly; Ivan cited roughly March 8 and November 1 and said to check each year. For multi-continent founders like Nuno, being off by an hour "is a disaster."
6. Checking only one calendar. Founders have personal and business calendars. Subscribe to all of them (+ > subscribe). Ivan overlays Pareto, personal (his wife books there), "architects", and partners' calendars.

### Booking calendars (live demo)
- Most events should come from booking links, not manual invites, to avoid conflicts. "That's the main reason a lot of entrepreneurs still don't use it. So, you jumping in, setting this up for them is, again, great success."
- **Google Calendar Appointment Schedule** (free; Create > Appointment schedule). "Google Calendar just took Calendly and all of these guys in the backyard..." Settings demoed:
  - Duration: sales call ~45 min; check-ins 15 min.
  - Availability: blocks remove slots.
  - Scheduling window: default 60 days. Ivan cuts it to 7-14 ("if somebody needs to book 60 days in advance then it's probably not the right fit").
  - Minimum notice.
  - Date-specific availability: Ivan prefers manual "Block" events with no detail needed. He asks Pareto RHs to block personal time immediately.
  - Buffer: combined before and after in Google.
  - Guest permissions, calendar and color, location (Google Meet auto-link), description, booking form with custom fields (e.g. "Budget", required), confirmation.
- **Parkinson's Law** (taught): "work expands to fill the time available for the completion." Thesis example: two months to do 3-4 days of work. Ivan keeps most events at 30 minutes or less, and his past hiring interviews were 15 minutes: "15 minutes is more than enough for me to understand if we are fit or not."
- **Calendly**: has a free tier, but paid plans lock features (multiple calendars, automations, reminders). Story: Calendly once locked the confirmation landing page, which "broke down all of the tracking."
- **GoHighLevel calendar** (what Ivan uses; "works better than the Google Calendar one"): custom look and forms, multiple staff, separate pre- and post-buffer, max bookings per day (e.g. 3), max bookings per slot, "Look busy" (pretend 60% of slots are taken), custom form before or after picking a date, consent, payments, notification emails for book/cancel/reschedule without a workflow, widget colors on-brand, embed code.
  - Most important for marketers: **redirect URL to a thank-you page** "that will tell you exactly what's next, what's going to happen... here's what you need to prepare. 1, 2, 3, 4, 5." (relevant to the funnel)

## Block 3: Project management

### Project vs task
- "It depends on time": dinner that takes 2 weeks is a project; one that takes an hour is a task. Projects need "much more time, resources, and energy."
- AI shifted the line. A landing page used to be a project and is now a task. Gaspar rebuilt a "free book" landing page in ~20 minutes, and paretotalent.com/workshop took about an hour.
- A real project: launching the Bootcamp (agenda, deck, landing page, funnel, pipeline, automation, ads, running ads).

### Framework: The 4 P's of a project (Problem, Plan, Progress, Present)
1. **Problem**: "What is it that you're actually working on?" Define and validate the real purpose first. "If there is no problem, guess what? There is no project." Running example (slides): "What would be the best insurance company for our business?" Insurance must fit "the business they run today, not the one they ran two years ago." Ask whether you even need to change. Story: Kasim "hated and loved" that Ivan questioned every problem. "Majority of the problems, he didn't have them. He imagined them... or saw somebody else doing that and decided we need to do the same."
2. **Plan**: who (named owners), by when (deadlines on every piece; "A plan with one end date and no checkpoints isn't a plan yet"), with what resources (budget, access, tools, people's time). Bootcamp example: Ampy for the agenda, Virginia for the community, Gaspar for ads; deadline before the start of October ("we need talent for October"); **$5,000**, GoHighLevel and a Claude subscription.
3. **Progress**: choose an output format (written reports, meetings, dashboards) and a channel (email, meetings, Slack). "A failure to communicate is a failure to perform." Without tracked progress the founder "will just think again that you are scamming them." Kasim's pain: people "doing amazing thing, biggest project ever, but they never actually said to anyone about that."
4. **Present**: "The deliverable's quality is the process's quality." Use the **Pyramid Principle** (slide): lead with the conclusion, then the reasoning, then the data. Insurance: "We recommend switching to Provider X, it saves 12% and actually covers contractors" (an illustrative slide example, not a real stat). Bootcamp deliverable: a folder with all ads, copy, ideas and built workflows with links.

### Framework: Work Breakdown Structure (WBS)
- "Hierarchical decomposition of the project into deliverables": tasks and subtasks leading to the outcome, shown as "a visual roadmap... not a wall of text."
- Insurance milestones (slide): policy audited → three quotes compared → founder review complete → new policy active.
- Story: in recent months Ivan had 3 calls with RHs in the pool "who were stuck. They received a task. That they think is a task, but it was a project."
- Live example: "social media app for founders" → code, brand, idea, features, marketing → language, devices, optimization, logo, colors, voice, competitor research, Google Trends... Decompose "up until you get the actual size that feels comfortable for you." How far depends on competency and resources.
- **Project creep**: scope uncovered mid-project (e.g. compliance adds a month). Better breakdown means more predictable output and pricing.
- **Budgeting method** (as taught): estimate hours per leaf task and sum them (e.g. 100 h). At 3 h/day that's 33 days, or about 7 weeks. Cost = hours × rate (100 h × $100 = $10,000), then "multiply it by 2. Just because... most likely you forgot a ton of things."
- AI-assisted WBS prompt (paraphrased from demo): "prepare me the work breakdown structure... break it down into bigger milestones... Ask me 5 questions that would allow you to narrow down the scope." Answer them to remove unknowns. Claude estimated 100 hours.

### ClickUp hierarchy (slides "The ClickUp vocabulary")
1. **Workspace**: the whole organization.
2. **Spaces**: by department, team, project or client (Ivan's: operations, marketing, admin, etc.).
3. **Lists**: tasks of similar type or outcome, inside Spaces or Folders.
4. **Tasks**: actionable units.
5. **Subtasks**: when a task is too big. "In Solutions 8, we had up to five levels of subtasks."
- Monday.com, Notion and Asana have the same structure. "All of these project management softwares, this is basically a big spreadsheet."
- Demo: Claude with the **ClickUp connector** built the whole "Entrepreneur Social Media App" list with milestones, subtasks, due dates, time estimates and dependencies, viewable as Kanban or Gantt. "That used to be the project management job, FYI. I know people who lost jobs because of that." Fallback: have AI generate a CSV and import it.

## Block 4: Reflect, then SOP

- Three reflection questions (slides): **What went well? What didn't? What did we learn?** "Instead of you trying to, like, YOLO every single thing... next time you're doing a project, you will do it better."
- **SOP (Standard Operating Procedure)**: "a document that centralizes how something is actually done." It's "a tutorial for yourself and for the people who will be working in the company."
- "Businesses built on, like, three things. On people, automations, and SOPs. If you have these three things done, then you will be able to have a business."
- SOP structure (as taught): **Purpose → Scope → Definitions/acronyms → Procedure** (step by step).
- Do's and don'ts (slide): DO state a clear purpose and make it interactive with a **Loom** walkthrough. DON'T assume or leave out details, and don't bury the structure so nobody can skim it.
- Pareto SOP examples (ClickUp docs, "Master SOPs"): iCloud calendar sharing for an assistant (made because RHs couldn't get into founders' iCloud calendars); credit card sharing via **privacy.com**; the Bootcamp process (welcome email with Slack invite, rules, agenda, peer groups of five, gamification, attendance link, survey, recording, homework). "We've already done it seven times... the first one, I didn't know what I didn't know. I didn't know that I need to have attendance link. I didn't know that I need to mute people by default."

## Objections & limiting beliefs (with answers taught)

- "We use Outlook" → migrate, or forward to Gmail, or use Spark Mail.
- "We already use Calendly / Microsoft 365" → adapt to their stack; propose changes later with a Loom.
